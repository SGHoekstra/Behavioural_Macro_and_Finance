\section{Comparison of Forecast Results} % Main chapter title

\label{Forecasts} % Change X to a consecutive number; for referencing this chapter elsewhere, use \ref{ChapterX}
For external validation of the agent based model, we conduct forecasting experiments where we compare out-of-sample forecast performance of the optimal consumption agent based model (OC-ABM), the baseline agent base model of \cite{poledna_economic_2019} (ABM), the extended heuristic agent based model (EH-ABM) with an unconstrained AR, VAR, ARX, and VARX model. We use the out-of-sample root mean squared error (RMSE)\footnote{The root mean squared error is defined as $RMSE = \sqrt{\frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^T(\hat{x}_t-x_t)^2}$. Here $\hat{x}_t$ is the forecasted value and $x_t$ is the observed value.} as the measure of forecasting performance. To compare whether the forecasts differ significantly in forecasting accuracy we use the modified Diebold-Mariano test \citep{harveyTestingEqualityPrediction1997}, which is corrected for the overall length of the forecasting horizon. The test is applied to the loss differential which is defined as the difference of the squared forecast errors. We use the forecast errors to assure that the compared error series are stationary. Furthermore, we adjust for the auto-correlation typically seen in multi-period forecast errors. It’s important to note that an efficient h-period forecast will have errors that follow a moving average process of order h-1 (MA(h-1)). To address this issue, we follow \cite{harveyTestingEqualityPrediction1997} and use a Newey-West estimator to calculate the sample variance of the loss differential. We compare real GDP, inflation, government consumption, exports, imports, and real GDP and inflation of the Euro area (EA), and finally the Euro Interbank Offered rate (Euribor).\\

The VAR(1) model is estimated over the sample of 1997Q1 to 2010Q1. The VAR(1) model is consequently re-estimated for every quarter from 2010Q1 to 2019Q4. The variables GDP, government consumption, exports, imports, and GDP of the euro area are entered as the first differences of the log of the variables. The variables inflation of the Netherlands and the inflation of the Euro area are entered as the first difference log of the GDP deflator. The Euribor rate is entered as the quarterly rate. We use the VAR(1) specification as this model specification minimized the Aikake and Bayesian information criteria. We calibrated the ABMs for the reference quarters 2012Q2 to 2019Q4 and use 100 Monte Carlo simulations. We calibrated the ABM with exogenous predictors for the reference quarters 2010Q1 to 2019Q4 and also use 100 Monte Carlo simulations.\\

In table \ref{table:rmse_validation} we can see the out-of-sample RMSE statistics for 1, 2, 4, 8, and 12 quarters over the period 2012Q1 to 2016Q4\footnote{We had to exclude the periods 2010Q1-2011Q4 as the CBS register data was not available for these periods and we had to exclude the periods 2017Q1-2019Q4 as this database was damaged due to computer issues.}. The RMSE statistics of GDP, government consumption, exports, imports and GDP of the Euro area are reported in log levels. The R MSE statistics of the variables inflation of the Netherlands and the inflation of the Euro area are reported as the first difference log of the GDP deflator. The RMSE statistic of the Euribor rate is reported as the quarterly rate. In the Diebold-Mariano test statistics are reported in the parentheses. We test whether the base version of the ABM and the adjusted policy rule version of the ABM have the same forecasting accuracy as the VAR(1) model. We observe that for most variables the forecasting RMSE of the agent based models is significantly lower than the forecasting accuracy of the VAR(1) model in the longrun. In the short run it seems that the VAR(1) performs better than the agent based models. Note however that the RMSE is very low for the VAR(1) model and that having relatively worse forecasting performance in terms of RMSE still results in a low forecasting RMSE.\\

\subsection{Conditional Forecasts}
We also test the forecasting performance by conditioning the forecasting of the models on the realized paths of real exports, real imports, and real government consumption. Conditional forecasts of the ABMs are realized by generating the realized paths of the exogenous variables. The expectations within the model are replaced with an ARX(1) rule instead of the AR(1) rule. For further explanation see section \ref{\label{sec:abmconditional}.\\ 

The VARX(1) model is estimated over the sample of 1997Q1 to 2010Q1. The VARX(1) model is consequently re-estimated for every quarter from 2010Q2 to 2016Q4. The variable GDP is entered as the first differences of the log of the variables. The variable inflation of the Netherlands is entered as the first difference log of the GDP deflator. We use the VARX(1) specification as benchmark since this model specification minimized the Aikake and Bayesian information criteria. 

In table \ref{table:rmse_validation_abmx} we can see that again the ABMs perform worse in short-term forecasting but perform better in long-term forecasting of gdp. The agent based models outperform the VARX(1) model in terms of short-term inflation forecasting. The OC-ABM is the best performing model of the all the agent based models in general. In table \ref{table:rmse_abmx} we compare the three ABMs with the ARX(1) model in forecasting GDP, inflation, household consumption, and investment. We can observe that the ABMs outperform the ARX(1) in forecasting investment. The ABMs outperform the ARX(1) model in long-term forecasting of GDP. The ARX(1) model has better short-term forecasting performance of household consumption.

\subsection{Decompositions of agent based model}
 We would like to stress that the model is stock-flow consistent while also complying the rules and conventions of national accounting (ESA). The model adheres to the double-bookkeeping principle for all forecasts. This implies that every flow within the model is an outflow for one agent (use of funds) and an inflow for another agent (source of funds). This means that we keep track of all in- and outflows of all the agent. As a consequence, we can decompose model in any way we want. In figure \ref{fig:national_accounting} we show this by the decomposition of GDP using the production, income, and expenditure definition of GDP. We can decompose the forecasts on sectoral level as we did with the previous forecasting test. Moreover, we can decompose GDP by seeing how income is distributed over the economy or how income is consumed over the economy.
